Did the children get it?

Every quiz here was written from a real lesson recording. The teacher taught, the system read the transcript back, listed the objectives the lesson actually covered, and asked the children about those — not about a syllabus. Then the children did something nobody had asked them to.

77.9%

of the objectives they were asked about, children mastered — every question on that objective right. 2,719 child-and-objective pairs, from 583 finished quizzes.

That number was 78.7% after the first hundred children and 78.6% after four hundred. It has not moved. Question by question the figure is higher — 85.3% of answers were right, the median child scored 88% — but the stricter reading is the useful one: a child who misses one of the two questions on fractions as parts of a whole has not mastered it, however good the score looks.

The quiz the teacher sent turned out to be the beginning, not the end.

When a child finishes, the assistant asks whether they want another. Nobody expected much of that offer. Of the 369 children who saw it and answered, 129 said yes — 45%. And the offer is the smaller half of it: children also came back on their own, through links their teacher had sent days before, or that a friend had forwarded.

On Tuesday she found a second Urdu quiz — synonyms — fumbled the first two questions, restarted, and got everything right; then did it again; then, hours later, once more. In between she read two English poems about the city and the farm for a grade-5 quiz, and finished the day on Three States of Matter.

Five different quizzes. Four subjects. Fifty-five of fifty-eight answers right. Nobody sent her the last four.

Left to themselves, what do they pick?

Of the 278 quizzes children chose on their own, English led, then maths and science. They do not stay in their lesson, and they do not stay in their year: 78 sittings were on grade 6–8 material, 45 on grades 1–2. They reach up and down.

Send it to a friend

After the scorecard, one question: Want to send this quiz to a friend? I'll tell you how they did once they finish. Over two days, 1,064 children saw it. 278 forwarded it, one in four. 154 friends actually opened the link, and 101 of them — two in three — finished the whole quiz. 98 times, the result went back to the child who sent it.

Where the questions get hard

Every question is written at a level — remembering, understanding, or applying. Apply questions cost every year something, and only a little: the gap is three points, not the six it looked like on day one.

How much weight this carries

  • Two days, 583 finished quizzes, 916 children. The headline has held between 77.9% and 78.8% as the sample grew from a hundred children to nine hundred; the cuts beneath it are less settled, and one of them already washed out.
  • The children are self-selected. They are the ones whose parents opened a WhatsApp link. 131 of 311 quizzes ever reached a child — the biggest loss in the whole chain, and it sits after everything that has been fixed.
  • Nobody was watching them answer. A quiz done at home can be done with help — which is also, sometimes, the point.
  • Depth counts different quizzes only. The 560 restarts are excluded throughout.
  • Zara is real; her name is not. No child's name, class or number appears on this page.
