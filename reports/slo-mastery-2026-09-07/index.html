A quiz from the lesson just taught

A teacher forwards one link to the class group, and the children take a quiz written from the lesson that teacher just taught. Until 6 October the quiz ran inside the WhatsApp chat, one message per step, about 46 for each finished quiz; on 1 October Meta began charging for every one of them. Since 7 October the same link opens a web page inside WhatsApp. Nothing is sent per question, and the page does what the chat could not: a voice for every question in English or Urdu, class and school leagues, a friend challenge, a video library and short reading and number games.

Why it moved

The quiz first ran inside the WhatsApp chat. A question was a message, every answer was a reply, and the feedback, the scorecard and the offers after it were more messages: about 46 for one finished quiz. Through September that cost nothing: replies a business sends inside a conversation the person has started were free.

On 1 October that changed. Meta now gives each number 1,000 free replies a month and charges for the rest; in Pakistan it is US$0.015 a message. A number like NIETE’s uses the free thousand within the first day of the month. At that rate the chat quiz costs about 67 cents for every finished quiz.

WhatsApp has a better place for a quiz than the chat: a link opens in WhatsApp’s own browser without leaving the app. So the link still travels the way it always did, from teacher to class group to a family phone, and everything after the tap happens in a page that sends no messages. All in, a finished quiz on the page costs two to four cents; the recorded voices and the one message that carries the link are nine-tenths of that.

Saving the messages was the reason. What the page opened up is the rest of this story.

Links sent before the switch still open the old way: all 456 WhatsApp-message sittings since 03:08 came from them. Every link sent after it opens the page. Of the web sittings, 71% were finished, against 53% of the message sittings in the same hours. They are different quizzes and different children, so that gap is a question to follow, not a finding.

410 of the web sittings were in English and 311 in Urdu. 129 of the 496 children were matched to their class’s list, so their results land against the right name in the teacher’s register.

The library

The video library opened to every child at 12:28. By 18:00, children had looked at 1,856 library screens, picked 158 videos, saved 71 to the phone and started 79 quizzes from a video they picked themselves.

Sharing

Children tapped a share button 1,074 times: 652 from the scorecard, 187 from the class league, 209 to challenge a friend and 26 from the school league. 498 friend challenges were made, and 24 quizzes have been played through one so far.

Not found yet

The hub and Jugnu’s Challenge went live for every child at 12:28 and 12:39. By 18:00 no child had opened either, apart from our own checks: the hub opens only when a child sends /quiz to the NIETE number, and nothing points children there yet. Zero is the honest number for now; this is where the next work is.

How far it has reached

Since the first quiz went out on 6 September, 8,831 different children have sat at least one, sent by 1,684 different teachers. Both lines are still climbing every day. They are climbing more slowly than in the first week: the most children seen for the first time on one day was 638, on 9 September; 2 October brought 203, and new teachers per school day have gone from over a hundred to 38.

Where the children come from

Every teacher belongs to a school, and every ICT school to one of six sectors, so both the teachers and the children can be placed. Across the six, 22.7% of the 6,729 teachers on the roll have sent at least one quiz, from 321 of the 433 schools. Sihala leads on penetration (31.1% of its teachers); Urban-I carries the most children (3,907) because it is the biggest sector. Mastery barely moves between them — Urban-I at 75.6%, Urban-II at 70.7% — which is the point: the quiz is written from the lesson, and the lessons are the same curriculum everywhere.

How fast it is growing

A child counts as new on the day of their first quiz ever; a teacher on the day they first receive a quiz with a class link. Since the launch, 8,620 children and 1,643 teachers have arrived that way. (Another 286 children and 49 teachers had used the ready-made video quizzes before 6 September and are not counted as new.)

With the week of 28 Sep complete: 1,722 new children in the week of 21 Sep, 1,600 in the week of 28 Sep (-7%); 340 new teachers, then 308 (-9%). 1 Oct falls inside the week of 28 Sep and was undercounted: WhatsApp did not deliver the bot's messages for part of that afternoon. On a bigger base the totals grow more slowly in percentage terms: about 4% more children a school day in the week of 28 Sep, against 50% and more in the first days.

One day stands out. On 25 Sep, 120 teachers received their first quiz — the most since 10 Sep — and 79 of them came through the lesson-plan offer that went out that afternoon. New children did not jump the same day (388, 91 of them from a lesson-plan quiz): the classes of those teachers had one evening, and then a weekend.

The weekend of 26–27 Sep

Saturday and Sunday are not school days, so a weekend is set against the other weekends, not against the week. Over 26–27 Sep 1,168 children sat a quiz (792 on Saturday, 481 on Sunday), 2,819 sittings in all, and 458 children took their first quiz ever. That is the biggest weekend so far on all three counts; the weekend before had 947 children, 2,183 sittings and 349 new children. The lesson-plan quizzes from Friday afternoon made the difference: 413 children sat one over the weekend (783 sittings), while sittings on coaching quizzes were lower than the weekend before (1,234 against 1,744). New teachers are few on any weekend, because teachers send quizzes on school days: 21 this time (19 on Saturday, 14 of them by a lesson-plan quiz), against 6, 9 on the earlier weekends. Where it lands

Two funnels, because two different things are being counted. A quiz is offered to one teacher; a quiz that is sent reaches a whole class. The wall is neither authoring nor delivery — it is a teacher deciding, on a given afternoon, that this lesson is worth a quiz.

Past that wall the chain holds: 93% of accepted quizzes are written and sent, and 1845 of them have been opened by at least one child. What happens to those children is the rest of this page.

The same quiz again

2,779 children have finished a quiz and then sat that same quiz again — 31% of everyone reached, across 7,456 repeat sittings. It happens every day, weekends included. The class report and the class card count each child once, using their latest attempt, so a child who repeats a quiz to improve is ranked on the result they ended with.

How much weight this carries

  • The web numbers are a few school hours. They run from 03:08 to 18:00 on 7 October. A morning is not a week, and the afternoon lesson-plan offers had not gone out yet.
  • Our own testing is taken out. The team checked the live service on its first morning. Those test children were deleted from the records, and their clicks are removed from the event counts by quiz code.
  • A child on the page is a new record. The page keeps its own record of who is playing, so a child who played on WhatsApp in September counts as new here. Returning children across the switch cannot be counted yet.
  • Children per day adds the two routes. A child who played both an old link and a new one on 7 October counts twice in that bar.
  • The children are self-selected. They are the ones whose families opened a link. Nobody was watching them answer.
  • Every name on this page is invented. The screens come from our test copy of the service; no child’s or teacher’s name, class or number appears here.
